  • Chapter 12: Stoichiometry

  • 1. Chemical and Mathematical Equations

  • 1. Equations

  • * A balanced chemical equation provides the same kind of quantitative information that a recipe does.

  • * Chemists use balanced chemical equations as a basis to calculate how much reactant is needed or product is formed in a reaction.

  • * A balanced chemical equation can be interpreted in terms of different quantities, including numbers of atoms, molecules, or moles; mass; and volume.

  • * Mass and atoms are conserved in every chemical reaction.

      • 1. Chemical equations show how many reactants will yield a certain amount of products
      2. Stoichiometry: Study of how quantities are calculated in chemical reactions
      3. Balanced chemical equations have different meanings depending on what scale they are viewed
  • 2. Atoms
      • 1. Balanced equations show the number and type of each atom in the reactants and products
      2. Neither of those change throughout the reaction
  • 3. Molecules
      • 1. Balanced equations show the ratio of atomic moles that combine to form a certain molecule
      2. Increasing the ratio yields more molecules

  • (1:3:2 = 1 molecule || 10:30:20 = 10 molecules)

  • 4. Moles
      • 1. Balanced equations show how many moles of reactants and products there are
      2. Indicated by the coefficients
      3. Often not the same throughout the reaction
      4. Helps you figure out how many moles of products or reactants there will be
  • 5. Mass
      • 1. Law of Conservation of Mass
      2. Reactants and products cumulatively have the same mass
  • 6. Volume
      • 1. Number of moles shows the volume of STP gases
      2. Often not the same throughout the reaction

  • 2. Chemical Calculations

  • 1. Mole Ratios

  • * In chemical calculations, mole ratios are used to convert between moles of reactant and moles of product, between moles of reactants, or between moles of products.

      • 1. Mole Ratio: Conversion factor between moles made with the coefficients
      2. New way of doing Dimensional Analysis
  • 2. Other Ratios

  • * In a typical stoichiometric problem, the given quantity is first converted to moles. Then the mole ratio from the balanced equation is used to calculate the number of moles of the wanted substance. Finally, the moles are converted to any other unit of measurement related to the unit mole, as the problem requires.

      • 1. One mole can be equal to many different things:
      2. 6.022x1023 representative particles of another mole
      3. 22.4 liters of another gaseous mole at STP
      4. Mass of another mole
      5. Go look at some example problems for more information

  • 3. Percent Yield and Limiting Reagent

  • 1. Excess and Limiting Reagents

  • * In a chemical reaction, an insufficient quantity of any of the reactants will limit the amount of product that forms.

      • 1. Unbalanced equations may contain more or less reactants than necessary
      2. Reagent: Substance used in a chemical reaction
      3. Limiting Reagent: Substance that determines the minimum amount of product that can be produced in a reaction
      4. Can only be found by converting the problem’s units into moles
      5. Excess Reagent: Surplus substance that does not undergo reaction
  • 2. Percent Yield

  • * The percent yield is a measure of the efficiency of a reaction carried out in the laboratory.

      • 1. Nothing is really 100% certain in life
      2. Theoretical Yield: Maximum amount of products that could be formed by the reactants
      3. Actual Yield: Amount of products formed by reactants in an experiment
      4. Percent Yield: Ratio of the theoretical yield to the actual yield, expressed as a percentage
      5. Similar to the accepted and experimental values in error calculations
      6. Percentage yields should not be higher than 100%
      7. Percent Yield = (Actual Yield / Theoretical Yield) X 100
  • 3. Reasons for Low Percent Yields
      • 1. Partially-completed reactions
      2. Impure reactants
      3. Loss of product
      4. Unintended side reactions
      5. Unwanted products
      6. Inaccurate measurements

  • Chapter 13: States of Matter

  • 1. Nature of Gasses

  • 1. Aesthetics of Kinetics

  • * The particles in a gas are considered to be small, hard spheres with an insignificant volume.

      • 1. Kinetic Energy: Energy of an object in motion
      2. Kinetic Theory: All objects and particles are constantly in motion
      3. Gas particles are spread out from each other
      4. Lots of empty space
      5. Few intermolecular forces at play
      6. Gasses fill up their space
  • 2. Random Number Gasses

  • * The motion of the particles in a gas is rapid, constant, and random.

  • * All collisions between particles in a gas are perfectly elastic.

      • 1. Particles are autonomous and each move on their own
      2. Brownian Motion: Linear, random movements of small particles
      3. Collide with other particles to get from Point A to Point B
      4. Total kinetic energy in the system remains constant and is fully conserved when particles collide (perfectly elastic)
  • 3. The Pressure is On (Alternate Title: Wait of the World)

  • * Gas pressure is the result of simultaneous collisions of billions of rapidly moving particles in a gas with an object.

      • 1. Lots of tiny particle collisions cause pressure to build
      2. Gas Pressure: Force exerted by a gas along the surface of an object
      3. Vacuum: Completely empty space with no matter or pressure
      4. Atmospheric Pressure: Global pressure caused by atmospheric particles
      5. Barometer: Device that measures atmospheric pressure using a column of mercury
  • 4. Conversion Factors
      • 1. 1 atm (atmospheric pressure)
      2. 14.7 psi (pounds per square inch)
      3. 101.3 kPa (newtons per square meter (very small amount of pressure, x1000))
      4. 760. mm Hg (height that the mercury reaches in a barometer under atmospheric pressure)
  • 5. Kinetic Energy

  • * The Kelvin temperature of a substance is directly proportional to the average kinetic energy of all the particles of the substance.

      • 1. Particles absorb energy as they get hotter
      2. Kinetic energy is inconsistent among particles of a sample
      3. Colder molecules are higher in number, but have lower average kinetic energy
      4. Hotter molecules are lesser in number, but have higher average kinetic energy
      5. Particles stop moving at Absolute Zero (0 K (-273.15° C))
      6. Kelvins measure the average kinetic energy of a sample
      7. [Graph 13.3]

  • 2. Nature of Liquids

  • 1. Model for Liquids

  • * The interplay between the disruptive motions of particles in a liquid and the attractions among the particles determines the physical properties of liquids.

      • 1. Intermolecular forces weakly hold particles together
      2. Definite volume
      3. Hardly affected by increases in pressure or compression (compressed state)
  • 2. Evaporation

  • * During evaporation, only those molecules with a certain minimum kinetic energy can escape from the surface of the liquid.

      • 1. Vaporization: Conversion of a liquid into a gas or vapor
      2. Evaporation: Vaporization occurring below the boiling point
      3. High-KE particles moving towards the surface of the liquid can overcome IMF and evaporate
      4. Increasing the temperature will increase the amount and average KE evaporating particles
  • 3. Vapor Pressure

  • * In a system at constant vapor pressure, a dynamic equilibrium exists between the vapor and the liquid. The system is in equilibrium because the rate of evaporation of liquid equals the rate of condensation of vapor.

      • 1. Vapor Pressure: Force exerted by a gas above a liquid
      2. Condensation builds when particles try to evaporate in a sealed container
      3. When particles evaporate, an equal amount will condense
      4. Vapor pressure increases with the temperature
      5. Manometer: Device that measures vapor pressure
  • 4. Boiling Point

  • * When a liquid is heated to a temperature at which particles throughout the liquid have enough kinetic energy to vaporize, the liquid begins to boil.

      • 1. Boiling Point: Temperature where the vapor pressure and external pressure on a liquid are equivalent
      2. Boiling is faster than evaporation and occurs at a set temperature
      3. Places with high altitudes have lower atmospheric pressure and lower boiling points
      4. Prevents you from boiling water on a mountaintop to make pasta
      5. Normal Boiling Point: Boiling point of a liquid at 101.3 kPa/1 atm/14.7 psi/760. mm Hg

  • 3. Nature of Solids

  • 1. Model for Solids

  • * The general properties of solids reflect the orderly arrangement and fixed locations of their particles.

      • 1. Tightly-packed particles
      2. Vibrate in place
      3. Compressed state
      4. Melting Point: Temperature where a solid changes into a liquid
      5. Particles vibrate violently enough from the added heat to break their bonds
      6. Same temperature as the freezing point
  • 2. Crystalline Solid Structures

  • * The shape of a crystal reflects the arrangement of the particles within the solid.

      • 1. Crystal: Solid substance with particles in an orderly, recurring lattice pattern
      2. Ionic crystals have high melting points
      3. Molecular crystals have low melting points
      4. Unit Cell: Group of particles in a crystal that demonstrate the geometry of the crystal
      5. Fourteen different kinds
  • 3. Non-Crystalline Solid Structures
      • 1. Amorphous Solid: Solid without an ordered internal structure
      2. Glass: Transparent, non-crystalline solid made from supercooled inorganic substances
      3. No definite melting point
      4. Soften instead of melting
  • 4. Allotropes
      • 1. Allotrope: Different molecular forms of the same element in the same state of matter
      2. Different physical properties
      3. Graphite, diamonds, and soot
      4. Only found in a few other nonmetals

  • 4. Changes of State

  • 1. Sublimation

  • * Sublimation occurs in solids with vapor pressures that exceed atmospheric pressure at or near room temperature.

      • 1. Sublimation: Conversion of a solid directly to a vapor
      2. Happens because of high vapor pressure in solids
  • 2. Phase Diagrams

  • * The conditions of pressure and temperature at which two phases exist in equilibrium are indicated on a phase diagram by a line separating the phases.

      • 1. Phase Diagram: Graph that shows at which state of matter a substance is in at a given temperature
      2. Shows normal melting, boiling, and triple points
      3. Allows you to identify the state of matter of a substance using its temperature and pressure
      4. Equilibrium: State where the properties of a system are static and not changing
      5. Solid lines dividing states indicate equilibriums
      6. Triple Point: Point on a phase diagram where all three states are at equilibrium

  • Chapter 14: Gasses

  • 1. Properties of Gasses

  • 1. Compressibility

  • * Gases are easily compressed because of the space between the particles in a gas.

      • 1. Gasses expand to fill their space (KMT random walks)
      2. Able to be compressed
      3. Compressibility: Measure of how much the volume of matter gets reduced when under pressure
      4. Lots of empty space between gas particles
      5. Forced together when compressed or cooled
  • 2. Variables of Gas Pressure

  • * The amount of gas, volume, and temperature are factors that affect gas pressure.

      • 1. PV = nRT
      2. P: Pressure (kPa)
      3. V: Volume (Liters)
      4. n: Number of moles (Moles)
      5. R: Gas pressure constant (explained later)
      6. T: Temperature (Kelvins)
      7. Increasing either the temperature or the number of moles will cause pressure to increase (directly proportional)
      8. Increasing the volume of thecontainer will cause pressure to decrease (inversely proportional)

  • 2. I Am the Law

  • 1. Boyle’s Law: Pressure and Volume

  • * If the temperature is constant, as the pressure of a gas increases, the volume decreases.

      • 1. Boyle’s Law: While moles and mass are constant, the volume and pressure of a gas are inversely proportional
      2. Invented by Robert Boyle
      3. P1 ✕ V1 = P2 ✕ V2
      4. Results are always the same at a given temperature
  • 2. Gay-Lussac’s Law: Pressure and Temperature

  • * As the temperature of an enclosed gas increases, the pressure increases, if the volume is constant.

      • 1. Lussac’s Law: While moles and volume are constant, the temperature and pressure of a gas are proportional
      2. P1 / T1 = P2 / T2
  • 3. Charles’ Law: Temperature and Volume

  • * As the temperature of an enclosed gas increases, the volume increases, if the pressure is constant.

      • 1. Charles’ Law: While moles and pressure are constant, the volume and temperature of a gas are proportional
      2. V1 / T1 = V2 / T2
      3. Should only be used with Kelvin units
  • 4. Combined Gas Law

  • * The combined gas law allows you to do calculations for situations in which only the amount of gas is constant.

      • 1. Combined Gas Law: Combination of the above three gas laws
      2. (P1 ✕ V1) / T1 = (P2 ✕ V2) / T2
      3. Helps you figure out what factors influence the properties of a gas and by how much
      4. Only works if the amount of gas moles is constant
      5. Can be converted into any one of the other law equations by moving around the variables

  • 3. Ideal Gases

  • 1. Ideal Gas Law

  • * Calculating the number of moles of a contained gas requires an expression that contains the variable, n.

      • 1. Ideal Gas Law: Upgraded version of the Combined Gas Law that includes all four variables
      2. PV = nRT
      3. Allows you to find the number of gas moles (n) while all the other variables are constant
      4. Proportional to volume; ratio of 1
      5. Ideal Gas Constant (R) : 8.31 kPa/0.0821 atm/62.36 mm Hg/1.21 psi
  • 2. Get Real

  • * Real gases differ most from an ideal gas at low temperatures and high pressures.

      • 1. Ideal gases perfectly abide by KMT laws:
      2. No intermolecular forces among particles
      3. No volume in particles
      4. Real gas particles have some IMF and volume
      5. Capable of condensing or solidifying when compressed or cooled
      6. More similar to ideal gases when at a higher temperature

  • 4. Mixtures and Movements

  • 1. Dalton’s Law

  • * In a mixture of gases, the total pressure is the sum of the partial pressures of the gases.

      • 1. Gas pressure is not affected by the number of different types of gases
      2. Partial Pressure: A percentage of pressure contributed by each gas in a mixture, contributing to the gas pressure as a whole
      3. Dalton’s Law of Partial Pressures: While volume and temperature are constant, the total pressure exerted by a mixture is equal to the partial pressures of each of its gasses
      4. Ptotal = P1 + P2 + P3 + P…
      5. Whenever the total gas pressure changes, the partial pressures will change
      6. Total gas pressure will change if the percent composition of the mixture changes
  • 2. Graham's Law

  • * Gases of lower molar mass diffuse and effuse faster than gases of higher molar mass.

      • 1. Diffusion: Movement of particles from a high concentration to a low concentration until an equilibrium is formed
      2. Effusion: Diffusion of gases through small holes in containers
      3. Type of particle is important
      4. Graham's Law of Effusion: The effusion rate of a gas is inversely proportional to the square root of its molar mass
      5. Related to its speed and kinetic energy (KE = ½mv2)
      6. Mass and velocity are inversely proportional
  • 3. Comparing Effusion Rates
      • 1. Lighter molecules move faster and will escape containers faster than heavier ones
      2. Bigger molecules move slower
      3. Containers at the same temperature will contain particles with the same kinetic energy
      4. Effusion rates are inversely proportional
      5. RateA / RateB = √(molar massB / molar massA)
  • 4. Bonus: Henry’s Law
      • 1. Henry’s Law: The amount of gas that dissolves in a liquid is proportional to the pressure of the gas over the liquid

  • Chapter 15: Water and Aqueous Systems

  • 1. Water and its Properties

  • 1. Water in the Liquid State

  • * Many unique properties of water---including its high surface tension and low vapor pressure---result from hydrogen bonding.

      • 1. Water exists in many different forms and states
      2. Polar covalent molecule
      3. Dipole attractions
      4. Forms hydrogen bonds
      5. Can absorb lots of energy
      6. Very cohesive and adhesive
  • 2. Surface Tension
      • 1. Molecules inside a water mass exert equal attractions on all sides
      2. Molecules on the surface of a water mass do not pull on each other equally
      3. Stronger bonds with other surface molecules
      4. Surface Tension: Inward force that reduces the surface area of a liquid
      5. Ability to resist change
      6. Water’s surface tension is higher than that of other liquids
      7. Surfacant: Substance that meddles with hydrogen bonding and decreases surface tension
  • 3. Pressure of Vapor
      • 1. Vapor Pressure: Pressure exerted by a vapor on the surface of its gaseous or liquid forms
      2. Determines when the liquid changes state
      3. Water has an unusually low vapor pressure
      4. Hydrogen bonds slow down evaporation and cause less molecules to evaporate
  • 4. SSD Water

  • * The structure of ice is a regular open framework of water molecules arranged like a honeycomb.

      • 1. Solid water is less dense than its liquid form
      2. Will float
      3. Molecules arranged in hexagonal patterns
      4. More energy needed to melt ice

  • 2. Homogeneous Aqueous Systems

  • 1. Here’s the Solution

  • * A solvent dissolves the solute. The solute becomes dispersed in the solvent.

      • 1. Homogeneous substances
      2. Aqueous Solution: Water containing substances dissolved in it
      3. Solvent: Substance in greater quantity that dissolves a solute
      4. Solute: Substance in lesser quantity that gets dissolved in a solvent
      5. Either can be any state of matter
      6. Ionic and polar-covalent compounds dissolve the fastest
      7. Nonpolar covalent molecules and alloys will not dissolve in water
  • 2. Solvation or Salvation

  • * As individual solute ions break away from the crystal, the negatively and positively charged ions become surrounded by solvent molecules and the ionic crystal dissolves.

      • 1. Polar regions of water molecules attract and break off the oppositely-charged ions of the solid ionic solutes
      2. Solvation: Process where positive and negative ions of a solid get surrounded by molecules of the solvent
      3. Ionic compounds with strong enough attractions can resist solvation
      4. Polar substances dissolve other polar substances
      5. Nonpolar substances dissolve other nonpolar substances
  • 3. Gators’ Aide

  • * All ionic compounds are electrolytes because they dissociate into ions.

      • 1. Electrolyte: Compound that can conduct electricity while in liquid form or while immersed in an aqueous solution
      2. Mobile ions
      3. Nonelectrolyte: Compound that does not conduct electricity while in liquid form nor while immersed in an aqueous solution
      4. Includes many molecular compounds that do not have ionic bonds
      5. Pure polar-molecular nonelectrolytes can become electrolytes when dissolved into ions
  • 4. Electrolyte Strength
      • 1. Some electrolytes conduct more electricity than others
      2. Strong Electrolyte: Electrolyte that sunders a solid entirely into its ion constituents and conducts a greater current
      3. Sodium chloride solution
      4. Weak Electrolyte: Electrolyte that does not do a good job at dissociating a solid into its ions and conducts a lesser current
      5. Ammonia, organic acids, and organic base solutions

  • 3. Hydrates

  • 1. Hydration

  • * In writing the formula of a hydrate, use a dot to connect the formula of the compound and the number of water molecules per formula unit.

      • 1. Hydrate: Compound that contains hydrated or crystalline water
      2. Has a defined number of water molecules per compound
      3. Example: CuSO4ᐧ5H2O has 5 water molecules per 1 copper (Ⅱ) sulfate compound (Copper (Ⅱ) sulfate pentahydrate)
      4. Will change composition and color when heated or cooled
  • 2. Efflorescent Hydrates
      • 1. Hydrogen bonds are not very strong
      2. Hydrates have a quantifiable vapor pressure
      3. Water in hydrates will effloresce or evaporate when its vapor pressure is greater than that of the surrounding air
      4. Efflorescence: Process of transforming into a powdery form when exposed to air
  • 3. Hygroscopic Hydrates
      • 1. Hygroscopic Compound: Compound that can remove moisture from air
      2. Desiccants
      3. Will add more water to the hydrate ratio formula
      4. You can use the mass of the water and the mass of the hydrate to determine the percentage of water present in the hydrate:
      5. % H2O = (mass of water / mass of hydrate) ✕ 100
  • 4. Deliquescent Compounds
      • 1. Very hygroscopic compounds
      2. Become wet when exposed to moist air
      3. Form solutions and dissolve completely
      4. Product solutions have a lower vapor pressure than the water vapor in the air

  • Chapter 16: Solutions

  • 1. Properties of Solutions

  • 1. Solution Formation

  • * The compositions of the solvent and the solute determine whether a substance will dissolve. Stirring (agitation), temperature, and the surface area of the dissolving particles determine how fast the substance will dissolve.

      • 1. Homogeneous mixtures
      2. Smaller solutes dissolve faster than larger solutes
      3. More surface area interacting with the solvent
      4. Agitation (stirring) will cause solutes to dissolve faster
      5. Does not affect the amount of solutes dissolved (only the speed)
      6. Hotter solvents will dissolve solutes faster than colder solvents
      7. Solvent molecules move faster and collide with the solutes with more force
  • 2. Solubility

  • * Solubility is often expressed in grams of solute per 100g of solvent.

      • 1. A Dynamic Equilibrium exists between particles dissolving (solvating) from a solid and particles crystallizing into a solid in a solution
      2. Prevents a solute from completely dissolving, even if more solute is added
      3. Only works if the temperature remains constant
      4. Saturated Solution: Solution with the regular, maximum amount of solute at a given temperature, pressure, and quantity of solvent
      5. Unsaturated Solution: Solution containing less solute than a saturated solution at a given temperature and pressure
      6. Solubility: Measure of the amount of solute that dissolves in a solvent to produce a saturated solution at a given temperature, pressure, and quantity of solvent (Measured in grams per liter)
      7. Miscible: Property of a pair of liquids that can dissolve in each other, regardless of their proportions
      8. Two polar liquids (water and ethanol), or two nonpolar liquids
      9. Immiscible: Liquids that are insoluble in each other
  • 3. Solubility and Temperature

  • * Temperature affects the solubility of solid, liquid, and gaseous solutes in a solvent; both temperature and pressure affect the solubility of gaseous solutes.

      • 1. Solids become more soluble when the temperature of the solvent increases (generally)
      2. Gases become less soluble when the temperature of the solvent increases
      3. Supersaturated Solution: Solution containing more solute than it should be able to at a certain temperature
      4. Causes the substance to crystallize, but not actually form any crystals (unless it gets scratched or a crystal seed is added to it)
      5. Rock candy
  • 4. Solubility and Pressure
      • 1. Changes in pressure affect the solubility of gases much more than the solubility of liquids and solids
      2. Increases with the partial pressure of the gases over the solution
      3. Carbonation
      4. Henry’s Law: The solubility of gas in a liquid and the pressure of the gas above the liquid are proportional while the temperature is constant
      5. S1 / P1 = S2 / P2

  • 2. Concentrations of Solutions

  • 1. Molarity, Morality, and Mortality

  • * To calculate the molarity of a solution, divide the moles of solute by the volume of the solution.

      • 1. Concentration: Measure of how much of a solute is dissolved in a quantity of solvent
      2. Dilute Solution: Solution containing a small amount of solute
      3. Concentrated Solution: Solution containing a large amount of solute
      4. Molarity (M): Number of solute moles dissolved in one liter of solution (quantitative)
      5. M = moles of solute / liters of solution
  • 2. Creating Dilutions

  • * Diluting a solution reduces the number of moles of solute per unit volume, but the total number of moles of solute in solution does not change.

      • 1. Solutions can be diluted by adding more solvent (water)
      2. Molarity remains constant before and after dilution
      3. Moles of solute = M1 ✕ V1 = M2 ✕ V2
  • 3. Percent Solutions

  • * The concentration of a solution in percent can be expressed in two ways: as the ratio of the volume of the solute to the volume of the solution or as the ratio of the mass of the solute to the mass of the solution.

      • 1. Molarity can be also expressed as a percentage
      2. If both the solute and solvent are liquids, then their volume can be compared
      3. Percent by volume (%(v/v)) = (volume of solute / volume of solution) ✕ 100
      4. If the solute is a solid, then the mass of the solvent and solute can be compared
      5. Percent by mass (%(m/m)) = (mass of solute / mass of solution) ✕ 100
      6. Units are %(v/v) or %(m/m)

  • 3. Colligative Solutions

  • 1. Lowering Vapor Pressure

  • * Three important colligative properties of solutions are vapor-pressure lowering, boiling-point elevation, and freezing-point depression.

  • * The decrease in a solution’s vapor pressure is proportional to the number of particles the solute makes in solution.

      • 1. Colligative Property: Property depending on the number of solute particles and not their identify (extensive)
      2. Tied to solute concentration
      3. Nonvolatile solutions are not easily vaporized and have a lower vapor pressure
      4. “Shells” of water reduce the number of solvent particles with enough KE to escape
      5. Ionic solutes that produce lots of ions when dissociated will cause the vapor pressure to decrease by an amount equal to the number of moles dissolved
      6. Vapor-Pressure Lowering: Dissolving more moles in a solution will decrease the vapor pressure by an amount scaling with the number of moles dissolved
  • 2. Depressing Freezing Points

  • * The magnitude of the freezing-point depression is proportional to the number of solute particles dissolved in the solvent and does not depend upon their identity.

      • 1. Solutes prevent liquid particles from forming an orderly pattern when freezing
      2. More KE needs to be lost for the solution to freeze over
      3. Solutions have lower freezing points than their pure solvent forms
      4. Freezing-Point Depression: Difference in temperature between the freezing points of a solution and its pure solvent
      5. Dissolving more moles of particles will cause the freezing point to decrease
      6. Antifreeze
  • 3. Elevating Boiling Points

  • * The magnitude of boiling-point elevation is proportional to the number of solute particles dissolved in the solvent.

      • 1. Liquids only boil when their vapor pressure is equal to the atmospheric pressure
      2. When nonvolatile solutes are added to a solution, the vapor pressure decreases
      3. More KE is needed to boil solvents with lots of solutes in them (and raise the vapor pressure high enough)
      4. Boiling-Point Elevation: Difference in temperature between the boiling point of a solution and the boiling point of of the pure solvent
      5. Fudge
      6. Abridged: Boiling points go up, everything else goes down (including the vapor pressure)

  • 4. Pages 491, 494--495

  • 1. Calculations Involving Colligative Properties (Molality and Mole Fractions)

  • * The unit molality and mole fractions are two additional ways in which chemists express the concentration of a solution.

      • 1. Molality (m): Number of solute moles dissolved in 1 kg of solvent
      2. Molality, the measure of moles dissolved in 1 kilogram (m), is different from molarity, the measure of moles dissolved in 1 liter (M)
      3. molality = moles of solute / kilogram of solvent
  • 2. Freezing-Point Depression and Boiling-Point Elevation Calculations

  • * The magnitudes of the freezing-point depression (ΔTf) and the boiling point elevation (ΔTb) of a solution are directly proportional to the modal concentration (m) when the solute is molecular, not ionic.

      • 1. The change in the freezing temperature (ΔTf) is the difference between the solution’s freezing point and the pure solvent’s freezing point
      2. The change in the boiling temperature (ΔTb) is the difference between the solution’s boiling point and the pure solvent’s boiling point
      3. Both are proportional with the number of moles dissolved
      4. Modal Freezing-Point Depression Constant (Kf): Constant equal to the change in freezing point for a 1-mole solution containing a nonvolatile molecular solute (measured in °C/m)
      5. ΔTf = Kf ✕ m (molality)
      6. Modal Boiling-Point Elevation Constant (Kb): Constant equal to the change in boiling point for a 1-mole solution containing a nonvolatile molecular solute (measured in °C/m)
      7. ΔTb = Kb ✕ m (molality)
      8. Tied to the number of ions produced by each compound’s formula unit

  • Chapter 17: Thermochemistry

  • 1. The Flow of Energy---Heat and Work

  • 1. Energy Transformations
      • 1. Energy: The ability to do work or supply heat
      2. Unlike matter, energy has no mass or volume
      3. Thermochemistry: Study of how energy changes during chemical reactions and changes in state
      4. Chemical Potential Energy: Energy contained within the chemical bonds of every substance
      5. The constituent atoms’ identities and arrangements will determine their CPE
  • 2. The Heat is On

  • * Heat always flows from a warmer object.

      • 1. Heat (q): Energy that transfers between objects with different temperatures
      2. Is transferred from hotter objects to colder objects
      3. Adding heat will increase the temperature of an object
      4. Heat transfer will stop once both objects’ temperatures are equal
  • 3. Exothermic and Endothermic Processes

  • * In an endothermic process, the system gains heat as the surroundings cool down.

  • * In an exothermic process, the system loses heat as the surroundings heat up.

      • 1. System: A group of objects that interact with each other
      2. Surroundings: Objects that exist outside of---but are physically adjacent to---the system
      3. Law of Conservation of Energy: Principle that energy is not created nor destroyed during any chemical or physical reaction
      4. When one object loses energy, another will gain an equal amount of energy
      5. Endothermic Process: Reaction where the system absorbs heat from the surroundings (positive q)
      6. Exothermic Process: Reaction where the system releases/produces/loses heat to its surroundings (negative q)
  • 4. Measuring Heat Flow

  • * Heat flow is measured in two common units, the calorie and the joule.

      • 1. Regular temperature units (Fahrenheit, Celsius, etc.) cannot be used
      2. Calorie (cal): Quantity of heat needed to raise the temperature of 1 gram of pure water by 1° C
      3. 1 Calorie (capitalized) is 1000 times larger than 1 calorie (lowercase) (1 Calorie = 1 kilocalorie = 1000 calories)
      4. Joule: SI unit of energy that raises the temperature of 1 gram of pure water by 0.2390° C
      5. 1 J = 0.2490 cal, 4.184 J = 1 cal
  • 5. Heat Capacity and Specific Heat

  • * The heat capacity of an object depends on both its mass and chemical composition.

      • 1. Specific Heat Capacity (C): Amount of heat needed to raise the temperature of an object by exactly 1° C
      2. Objects with greater heat capacity are less affected by changes in temperature
      3. Water, liquids, and heavy objects have higher specific heats
      4. Metals have low specific heats
      5. q = C ✕ m ✕ ΔT
      6. energy absorbed/released = specific heat ✕ mass ✕ change in temperature
      7. ΔT = Tf (final temperature) - Ti (initial temperature)

  • 2. Measuring and Expressing Enthalpy Changes

  • 1. Calorimetry

  • * In calorimetry, the heat released by the system is equal to the heat absorbed by its surroundings. Conversely, the heat absorbed by a system is equal to the heat released by its surroundings.

      • 1. Calorimetry: Precise measurement of the heat flow into or out of a system during chemical or physical changes
      2. Calorimeter: Device used to measure the absorption or release of heat during a chemical or physical reaction
      3. Good insulators
  • 2. Constant-Pressure Calorimeters
      • 1. Enthalpy (H): Property with a value equal to the heat content of a system at a constant pressure
      2. Changes in heat (ΔH)
      3. qsystem = ΔH = –qsurroundings = –m ✕ C ✕ ΔT
      4. qsurroundings = heat absorbed by surroundings, qsystem = heat absorbed by system, m = mass of water, C = specific heat of water
  • 3. Equations Thermochemical

  • * In a chemical equation, the enthalpy change for the reaction can be written as either a reactant or product.

      • 1. Thermochemical Equation: Chemical equation including the change in enthalpy
      2. Heat of Reaction: Change in enthalpy for the reaction
      3. Each mole converted during the reaction will produce or absorb a certain amount of energy
      4. Reactants and products must be in their regular states of matter at 25° C and the pressure must be 1 atm
      5. ΔH is positive for endothermic reactions, and negative for exothermic reactions
      6. Heat of Combustion: Heat produced or absorbed during a reaction per mole of substance completely burned

  • 3. Heat in Changes of State

  • 1. Heats of Fusion and Solidification

  • * The quantity of heat absorbed by a melting solid is exactly the same as the quantity of heat released when the liquid solidifies; that is, ΔHfus = –Δsolid.

      • 1. Temperature remains constant while a substance transitions between states
      2. Energy is used to rearrange atoms instead of increasing temperature
      3. Molar Heat of Fusion (ΔHfus): Heat absorbed by one mole of solid substance when melting into a liquid at constant temperature, inverse of ΔHsolid
      4. Molar Heat of Solidification (ΔHsolid): Heat lost by one mole of liquid substance when freezing into a solid at constant temperature, inverse of ΔHfus
      5. Water gains 6.01 kJ/mol when melting and loses 6.01 kJ/mol when freezing
  • 2. Heats of Vaporization and Condensation

  • * The quantity of heat absorbed by a vaporizing liquid is exactly the same as the quantity of heat released when the vapor condenses; that is, ΔHvap = –Δcond.

      • 1. Molar Heat of Vaporization (ΔHvap): Amount of heat necessary to vaporize one mole of liquid into a vapor, inverse of ΔHcond
      2. Tied to the liquids’ normal boiling points
      3. Molar Heat of Condensation (ΔHcond): Amount of heat necessary to condense one mole of vapor into a liquid, inverse of ΔHvap
  • 3. Heat of Solution

  • * During the formation of a solution, heat is either released or absorbed.

      • 1. Molar Heat of Solution (ΔHsoln): Change in enthalpy caused by the dissolution of one mole of substance

  • 1. Section 18.1: Rates of Reaction

  • 1. Collision Theory

  • * In chemistry, the rate of chemical change or the reaction rate is usually expressed as the amount of reactant changing per unit time.

      • 1. Rate: Measure of the speed of a change over an interval of time
      2. Collision Theory: High-KE atoms react to form products when colliding with each other
      3. Determines how fast reactions will be
      4. Atoms simply bounce off each other if they do not have enough KE
      5. Conversely, molecules will break apart and decompose if enough energy is supplied
  • 2. It’s Go Time
      • 1. Activation Energy: Minimum energy needed for colliding particles to react
      2. Big “bump” in reaction speed graphs
      3. Activated Complex/Transition State: Unstable arrangement of atoms briefly formed at the top of the “activation energy barrier” when the reaction is at its highest level of energy
      4. The arrangement will either transform forwards into the products or backwards into the reactants
  • 3. Factors Affecting Reaction Rates

  • * The rate of a chemical reaction depends upon temperature, concentration, particle size, and the use of a catalyst.

      • 1. The speed of a reaction can be altered by outside factors
      2. High temperatures increase reaction speed
      3. More frequent collisions and high-KE particles
      4. High concentrations increase reaction speed
      5. Crowded rooms
      6. Smaller particles increase reaction speed
      7. Dissolution
      8. Catalysts increase reaction speed
      9. Lowers the activation energy barrier
      10. Inhibitor: Evil substance interfering with catalysts

  • 2. Section 18.2: Reversible Reactions and Equilibrium

  • 1. Reversible Reactions

  • * At chemical equilibrium, no net change occurs in the actual amounts of the components of the system.

      • 1. Reversible Reaction: Reaction where reactants and products simultaneously convert into one another
      2. Indicated by two arrows
      3. The forwards and backwards reactions will accelerate at similar rates
      4. Chemical Equilibrium: Point in a reaction where the rates of each reaction are equal (plateaued)
      5. Concentrations remain constant, but both possible products will not be in equal quantities
      6. Equilibrium Position: Relative concentrations of reactants and products at equilibrium
      7. All reactions are hypothetically reversible
      8. Catalysts can help reactions reach equilibrium faster
  • 2. Châtelier’s Science Principles

  • * Stresses that upset the equilibrium of a chemical system include changes in the concentration of reactants or products, changes in temperature, and changes in pressure.

      • 1. When an equilibrium is disturbed, the system will try to regain equilibrium
      2. The amount of products or reactants might be different (equilibrium shift)
      3. Le Châtelier’s Principle: When stress is applied to a dynamic system, the system will change according to the stress applied
      4. Found in reversible reactions
  • 3. Disruptions and Stressors
      • 1. Concentration
      2. When one concentration of a reactant or product decreases, another will increase
      3. Rates of reaction will go down, but then back up
      4. Temperature
      5. The equilibrium will shift to the direction that absorbs heat
      6. Pressure
      7. Only affects gaseous systems
      8. Increasing pressure will shift the equilibrium to the product side
  • 4. Equilibrium Constants

  • * A value of Keq greater than 1 means that products are favored over reactants; a value of Keq less than 1 means that reactants are favored over products.

      • 1. Equilibrium Constant (Keq): Ratio of product concentrations to reactant concentrations while at equilibrium
      2. Keq = ([C]c ✕ [D]d) / ([A]a ✕ [B]b)
      3. Concentrations C and D are products, and concentrations A and B are reactants
      4. Each concentration is amplified by its number of moles when balanced
      5. Keq > 1: Products favored at equilibrium
      6. Keq < 1: Reactants favored at equilibrium

  • 1. Oxidation and Reduction

  • 1. Reactions Redux

  • * The substance gaining oxygen is oxidized, while the substance losing oxygen is reduced.

      2. Oxidation-Reduction/Redox Reaction: Reaction where substances gain or lose electrons
      3. Rusting: 4Fe + 3O2 → 2Fe2O3
      4. Burning: C + O2 → CO2
      5. Bleaching
  • 2. Electron Shifts
  • * Losing electrons is oxidation. Gaining electrons is reduction.
      • 1. Atoms become more or less stable by gaining (reduction) or losing (oxidation) electrons
      2. Oxygen is the second most electronegative element
      3. Electrons get attracted to it more strongly than other elements (except fluorine)
      4. Even partial losses or partial gains of electrons can cause oxidation or reduction, like in covalent bonding
      5. Redox reactions produce lots of energy (exothermic)
      6. Oxidation: A complete or partial loss of electrons; gaining oxygen
      7. Reduction: A complete or partial gain of electrons; losing oxygen
      8. Reducing Agent: Reactant substance that loses electrons (oxidation) and causes the other substance to gain electrons (reduction)
      9. Oxidizing Agent: Reactant substance that gains electrons (reduction) and causes the other substance to gain electrons (oxidation)
      10. LEO says GER
  • 3. Corrosion

  • * Iron, a common construction metal often used in the alloy, steel, corrodes by getting oxidized into iron ions with oxygen.

      • 1. The speed of corrosion increases with the speed of electron transfers
      2. Substances increasing the speed of electron transfers will increase the speed of corrosion (water, salts, acids)
      3. Noble metals (gold and platinum) can resist having their electrons taken and do not tarnish
      4. Oxide coatings caused by initial corrosion can prevent further corrosion
      5. Oil, plastic, paint, or reducing agents (sacrificial metal) can prevent corrosion
  • 4. Oxidation Numbers

  • * As a general rule, a bonded atom’s oxidation number is the charge it would have if the electrons in the bond were assigned to the atom of the more electronegative element.

  • * An increase in the oxidation number of an atom or ion indicates oxidation. A decrease in the oxidation number of an atom or ion indicates reduction.

      • 1. Oxidation Number: Integer indicating the degree of oxidation or reduction in an atom
      2. Total number of electrons that an atom either gains or loses in order to form a chemical bond with another atom.
      3. 2. Balancing Redox Equations
  • 1. Identifying Redox Reactions
  • * If the oxidation number of an element in a reacting species changes, then that element has undergone either oxidation or reduction. Therefore, the reaction as a whole must be a redox reaction.
  • 1. Reactions where electrons get transferred are redox (single-replacement, combustion, combination, decomposition)
  • 2. Lightning, ignition, color changes
  • 3. Reactions without electron transfers are not redox (double-replacement, acid-base)
  • 2. Oxidation Number Balancing
  • * In a balanced redox equation, the total increase in the oxidation number of the species oxidized must be balanced by the total decrease in the oxidation number of the species reduced.
  • 1. The total number of electrons gained must be equal to the number of electrons lost during a reaction
  • 2. Keep track of electron transfers
  • 3. Oxidation-Number Change Method: Balancing a redox equation by identifying which elements’ oxidation numbers increase or decrease and by how much
  • 4. 1. Assign oxidation numbers to each element on either side
  • 5. 2. Identify which elements are oxidized or reduced
  • 6. 3. Connect the same elements on either side with brackets, and note the change in the oxidation number
  • 7. 4. Place coefficients with values equal to the factor needed to get from the reactant element’s oxidation number to the product element’s oxidation number. Make the total increase in oxidation number equal to the total decrease in oxidation number by using appropriate coefficients
      • 3. Half-Reaction Balancing
      * To balance a redox reaction using the half-reaction method, write separate half-reactions for the oxidation and the reduction. After you balance atoms in each half-reaction, balance electrons gained in the reduction with electrons lost in the oxidation.
      1. Best used when the same element appears multiple times on one side of the equation
      2. Reaction takes place in an acidic or basic solution
      3. Half-Reaction: Equation showing either just the oxidation or reduction reactions that occur in a redox reaction
      4. Half-Reaction Method: Individually balancing oxidation and reduction half-reactions, then combining the two back into the full equation
      5. 1. Write the unbalanced equation in ionic form (like with net ionic equations)
      6. 2. Separate the oxidation reactions into oxidation half-reactions and the reduction reactions into reduction half-reactions (ignore ions that do not get reduced or oxidized)
      7. 3. Balance each half-reaction, without balancing the charges
      8. If the reaction takes place in an acidic solution, then hydrogen ions and water can be added to balance the half-reaction
      9. If the reaction takes place in a basic solution, then hydroxide ions and water can be added to balance the half-reaction
      10. 4. Add electrons on either side if necessary to balance the charges
      11. 5. Multiply each half-reaction by a number that will make the number of electrons equal on both sides
